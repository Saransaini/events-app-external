The short version

VAULT in Plain English

An inflatable golf cage that stands up because the net and the frame hold each other up. Here is the whole idea without the engineering.

What it is

A golf practice cage you inflate with a pump in about ten minutes, hit balls into, then deflate and put in a bag. It comes in three sizes so it fits a normal garage, a home studio, or a school gym.

The one on the market you asked me to work around — the InMotion Air GX-6 — is basically an inflatable box: straight fat tubes making a rectangle, with a net hanging inside it. Yours is built a completely different way, which is the whole point.

The two ideas that make it different

Idea one

Bend the tubes into an arch instead of a box

Think of a pool noodle. Push down on the middle of a straight one and it folds in half. Now bend that same noodle into an arch, feet on the ground, and push down — it barely moves, because the force runs along the tube instead of trying to fold it. That is exactly why old stone bridges are arches.

Same trick here. Because the shape is doing the work, the arch version can be built from 8-inch tubes where a boxy frame would need about 13-inch tubes to survive the same hit. That is roughly a third less fabric — lighter to carry, cheaper to make, smaller to pack.

One refinement: the arch is flattened across the top rather than pointed, so you get full height across the whole area where the club actually swings, and it only drops away out at the sides where the club is near vertical anyway.

Idea two

Make the net part of the structure

In a normal cage the net just hangs there. It is dead weight the frame has to carry.

Here the net is pulled tight like a drum skin between the arches. The arches push outward, the net pulls inward, and neither one can move — the same way the strings and the frame of a tennis racket hold each other in shape. The net stops the arches wobbling sideways, and it spreads the shock of a ball across all three arches instead of one.

The nice consequence: indoors it needs no stakes, no guy ropes and no sandbags. All the pulling is between parts of the cage itself, so nothing gets transmitted to your floor. Weights are only needed outdoors, for wind.

Why there are two nets, not one

This trips people up, so: a net pulled tight enough to hold the frame steady would be like hitting into a drum-tight trampoline — the ball would come straight back at you. A net loose enough to catch a ball gently can’t hold anything up. You can’t have both in one net.

So there are two layers. A tight web of straps at the back does the structural job. A loose net in front does the catching. They even need opposite materials: the straps must not stretch, and the catching net must stretch, because the stretch is what softens the hit.

The number people get wrong

When the net catches a driver, it bulges forward about 20 inches. So you need roughly 2 feet of empty space between the front of the cage and your garage wall — otherwise the wall stops the ball, not the net. You can tighten the straps to shrink that gap if you’re cramped, but the ball then hits harder.

Will it fit your garage?

  • Single-car garage (usually about 12 ft wide inside) — V-9 only. The V-10 is 12 ft wide and would leave you nothing at the sides.
  • Two-car garage (20 ft or wider) — V-9 or V-10, comfortably.
  • Basement or home studio — the ceiling decides it, not the floor. Measure the ceiling first.
  • Gym, school hall, commercial bay — V-12.

The V-10 is the main model: it takes 96 sq ft of floor against the GX-6’s 60 sq ft, and weighs 62 lb against its 60 lb. Much bigger bay, same carry.

The mistake that will cost you a prototype

Searching for fabric will turn up banner and billboard PVC first. It looks identical, welds beautifully, and costs a third as much. It is far too thin: it will hold about 3 psi and yours needs 12. When you order, specify the weight per square metre and the strength, not the fabric name.

How you’d actually get one built

You can’t do the welding at home — airtight tubes need a radio-frequency welding machine, which is workshop equipment. Everything either side of it you can do yourself.

  1. Chalk the arch shape full-size on your driveway and stand inside it with a driver. Catches shape mistakes for free.
  2. Find a shop. Inflatable boat builders and repairers, inflatable advertising or event-tent makers, truck-tarp and awning shops. Search “HF welding PVC fabric” or “inflatable fabrication”.
  3. Have them build one arch first, not all three. It’s about a fifth of the cost and it proves the patterns and their weld quality. Test it at 18 psi for half an hour with soapy water on the seams before going further.
  4. Then the rest of the frame. Every tube gets leak-tested on its own before anything is assembled.
  5. Net assembly is sewing work — you or a canvas shop. Tedious but not skilled.
  6. First inflation: pump to half pressure, fit the net loose, then bring it up to full — the arch straightens as it inflates and tightens the net for you. Tighten the straps in a criss-cross order like car wheel nuts, never one after another around the edge.

Why the second is half price: most of the first one’s cost is figuring it out — making patterns, setting up the welder, re-doing the part that came out wrong. That work doesn’t repeat.

Why it costs more than the $3,500 GX-6: that’s normal and not a problem with your design. You’re paying a shop by the hour for a one-off; they’re making hundreds and spreading the setup across all of them. Materials are only about $1,250 of your $5,600 — that’s the floor that never drops much. The labour is what falls away with volume.

How long: 6–10 weeks, and most of that is sitting in the shop’s queue rather than anyone working on it.

Before you budget on this

These are estimates built from typical shop hourly rates, not quotes. Send the drawings to two or three shops and get real numbers before you commit to anything — welding labour is the biggest line and it varies a lot between shops.

Can you patent it?

Honest answer: maybe, but not on the arch alone. Inflatable arches have been used in military and event tents for decades, so “an inflatable arch holding a net” is not new and won’t get you a patent.

What might be new is the combination: the arch and the net holding each other up, plus splitting the net into a tight structural layer and a loose catching layer. That pairing is the thing worth putting in front of an attorney.

Two things they need to look at closely: the GX-6’s own pending applications (which may not be publicly searchable yet — applications stay hidden for 18 months), and an existing granted patent, US 7,118,487, “Inflatable sport ball arresting structure”, which is close enough that an examiner will almost certainly raise it.

I’m not able to tell you whether it’s patentable — that needs a proper search by a registered patent attorney. What the full document does is get you into that meeting with the design defined and the search already scoped, which is the expensive part to produce.

What to watch out for

  • Every number in the design is calculated, not measured. The maths is the right maths, but nothing has been built or tested yet. Build the prototype before you spend money on tooling or file the full patent.
  • The V-9 is honestly limited. A 9-foot ceiling constrains any cage, including this one. Publish the real height rating even though it costs sales — it prevents returns and angry customers.
  • The net will loosen. Fabric relaxes over the first half hour. Plan on re-tightening, and write it into the instructions.
  • Don’t bolt a projector to the frame. An inflatable moves a few millimetres as it warms up, and your picture will drift. Floor stand or ceiling mount only.
