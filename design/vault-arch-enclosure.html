VAULT Arch Enclosure A pneumatic barrel-vault golf cage in which the net is the bracing, not the cargo

Three models — garage, studio, academy — built on one structural idea: replace the inflated portal box with a three-centred pneumatic arch, and make the ball net a pre-tensioned structural diaphragm that braces it. Every load in the system closes on itself.

Written to be built from. Sheets 00–07 set the architecture and the numbers behind it; Sheets 08–10 are the handoff package — named materials, a full bill of materials, and a stage-by-stage prototype build with patterning, weld spec, budget and schedule. Sheet 11 is unit cost and pricing; Sheets 12–13 are for patent counsel and for the test rig.

The three numbers that set the frame

Thermal drift. A sealed chamber at 12 psi gauge and 20 °C, left in a garage that reaches 50 °C, rises to (12 + 14.7) × 323/293 − 14.7 = 14.7 psi. The relief valve is set at 16 psi so normal solar gain does not vent the structure — venting on a hot afternoon means re-pumping before every session, which is how sealed-air products earn a reputation for leaking.

Fabric utilisation. Hoop tension in a 229 mm tube at 12 psi is pR = 9.47 kN/m. A 1100 dtex PVC-coated polyester rated 60 kN/m at 80 % weld efficiency gives SF 5.1 on the fabric — the fabric is not the limit. The limit is the beam going slack in bending.

Wrinkling, not rupture. A pressure-stabilised tube fails by losing pre-tension on its compression side long before the fabric breaks. Onset is Mw = πpR³; for the V-10 arch (Ø229) that is 390 N·m, with axial capacity pπR² = 3,408 N. Every structural decision in this document is an attempt to keep demand well inside those two numbers — which is the entire argument for the arch.

SHEET 01

General arrangement — V-10 Studio

All dimensions mm unless noted
Arch centreline geometry shown

The vault runs front-to-back: two transverse arches, tied by two crown purlins and closed at the ground by a continuous base rail. Critically, the plane of each arch is the plane of the golf swing — so the structure’s height profile and the swing’s height profile are the same curve, and the frame stops carrying material into corners the club never reaches.

SHEET 02

Why an arch

Comparative load path · equal pressure, equal load

An inflated tube is a bad beam and an excellent strut. It has no material stiffness of its own — all of it is borrowed from internal pressure, and it evaporates the moment bending drives the compression fibre to zero. So the question is not “how big a beam?” but how do we stop asking the fabric to bend at all.

A funicular arch answers that. Under its design load an arch carries almost pure axial thrust, which is precisely the action a pressurised tube is good at. The comparison below applies the same net pre-tension and the same impact share to a portal and to an arch at the same 12 psi.

Read this comparison honestly

Panel A is not a claim that the GX-6 is oversized. Its net appears to hang as a liner rather than being pre-tensioned, so its frame is not asked to carry membrane tension — a legitimate design choice with its own logic. The comparison is between two frames both carrying a pre-tensioned net. That is the fair test, because pre-tensioning the net is what buys the diaphragm bracing and the flat screen plane on Sheet 03. The arch is what makes pre-tensioning affordable.

Profile selection — why three-centred and not parabolic

A parabolic or semicircular arch peaks at a point. A golf swing does not: the club-head apex wanders across roughly ±750 mm depending on the player’s height, plane and handedness. A single-radius arch that clears the apex at centreline will clip it 600 mm off centre.

The profile here is three-centred (basket-handle): a long crown radius R1 = 1.25W flanked by short haunch radii R2 = 0.31W. On the V-10 that holds the crown within 50 mm of full height across a ±898 mm band — a nearly flat ceiling exactly where the club needs one — then drops away steeply at the haunches, where the club is near vertical and the height is wasted. The result matches a portal frame’s usable clear height across the whole swing band while shedding the bending demand that sized the portal’s top member.

SHEET 03

The net as structure

Two-layer membrane · structural + arrest

The second half of the architecture. In a conventional cage the net is cargo: dead weight the frame carries. Here it is a pre-tensioned diaphragm that braces the arches out of plane, distributes a point impact across all three of them, and provides a flat planar reference for the screen module.

That creates a conflict, and resolving it is the whole design. A membrane stiff enough to brace is far too stiff to catch a ball gently — at zero travel the arrest force goes to infinity. So the two jobs are split across two layers that share one frame:

LayerJobSpecTension
Structural diagridBraces arches, distributes impact, carries screen25 mm PET webbing, MBS 11 kN, 600 mm pitch, laid on 45° bias800 N pre-tension
Arrest netStops the ball over 500 mm of travel#42 knotless nylon, 22 mm mesh, hung with 150 mm controlled slackslack — near zero
Screen module (opt.)Projection surface only4-ply knit polyester, laced on 6 mm shock cord @ 300 mmlight — optical only

The 45° bias matters more than it looks. A square mesh loaded in its own plane is a mechanism — it racks into parallelograms and carries nothing until it locks up. Rotate the grid 45° to the principal tension and the load runs axially down the webs instead. The diagrid is what turns a net from cargo into a shear panel.

What the diaphragm buys, quantified

  • Impact sharing. A point strike delivers 734 N. Through the diagrid it reaches the frame as roughly 45 % on the worst of two arches instead of 100 % — the difference between SF 1.2 and SF 2.8 at Ø229, i.e. the difference between a design that works and one that does not. With only two arches this distribution matters more than it did with three, which is why T3 is now the most load-bearing test in the plan.
  • Out-of-plane bracing. The arches are slender in their weak direction. The diaphragm ties all three together and to the base rail, so no guy lines, no outriggers, no extra footprint.
  • A flat plane for free. The screen laces to a surface that is already planar and pre-tensioned, so it needs no frame of its own — and, having no rigid frame edge, no abrasion line where screens normally fail first.
  • Two fibres that want opposite things. The structural layer must not stretch, so it is polyester. The arrest layer must stretch — nylon’s ~20 % elongation is doing real work absorbing the strike. No single fibre is right for both jobs, which is an independent reason the layers cannot be combined into one. See Sheet 08.
  • A self-closing load loop. Pre-tension runs arch → diagrid → base rail → arch. It never reaches the ground, so indoors the unit needs no ballast or anchorage at all. Ballast is required only for outdoor wind (Sheet 06).
SHEET 04

Pneumatic system

One air space · one valve · no plumbing

Deliberately dumb. Every chamber opens into every other chamber through a hole in the shared wall where two tubes are welded together — a Ø50 mm port at each arch foot and each purlin end. Air distributes itself. One valve fills the whole structure and the same valve dumps it. That is the entire system.

This replaced a more complicated design, on purpose

An earlier revision routed a 25 mm manifold hose inside the base rail with a one-way check valve at every limb, so a puncture in one chamber left the rest inflated. The redundancy was real. It was not worth what it cost: seven check valves, twelve metres of hose, about eight pounds, extra weld operations, and eight more joints that can leak — on a product whose whole appeal is that you pump it up and hit balls. Redundancy that adds eight failure points to protect against one is not redundancy.

What you give up, stated plainly: a puncture anywhere takes the whole cage down. That is an acceptable trade here. Nobody shelters under this, nothing falls on anyone, and a 12 psi structure with a small puncture sinks over minutes rather than seconds. You patch it like a bicycle tyre and pump it back up. If this were a shelter people slept in, the answer would be different.

On the relief valve — the one part worth arguing about

Sealed at 12 psi and 20 °C, a cage left in a garage that reaches 50 °C rises to (12 + 14.7) × 323/293 − 14.7 = 14.7 psi. If someone over-inflated to 15 to begin with, that becomes about 18. Against a minimum burst of 60 psi that is still better than 3:1, so the fabric does not care — which is why inflatable paddleboards ship without one.

Keep it anyway, but know why: it is a $20 part that protects against the customer who reads “12 psi” as a suggestion, pumps to 20, and leaves it in a hot car. It is insurance against user behaviour, not against physics. If cost or part count becomes critical, this is the first thing to delete.

One small consequence to check on test

With every chamber connected, air can shuttle between them when a ball strikes. That makes the frame marginally softer than isolated chambers would be, because the pressure rise from a deflected arch bleeds into the rest of the volume instead of being contained. The effect should be negligible — a pressurised tube gets nearly all its stiffness from fabric pre-tension, not from air compressibility — but it is a real difference, and T1 on Sheet 13 should measure the deflection rather than assume it.

SHEET 05

Impact, travel and standoff

The one number owners get wrong

Arrest travel is not a nuisance to be minimised — it is the design variable. Peak force falls as 1/δ, so every millimetre of travel you can afford buys frame margin, net life and quiet. What it costs is floor: the enclosure must stand off the wall by the travel plus a clearance margin, or the net simply hits the wall and the wall does the arresting.

Because the diagrid runs through ratchet tensioners rather than fixed loops, the owner sets this trade themselves at setup — a garage with 600 mm to spare tightens up and accepts a harder catch; an academy with room lets it out and gets a quieter cage that lasts longer.

Tipping, checked properly

A 734 N peak at 1.5 m looks alarming as a static overturning moment. It is not: the load is an impulse. The 4.11 N·s strike imparts about 0.05 J of rotational energy to the unit, against roughly 450 J needed to lift the centre of gravity over the front rail. Margin is four orders of magnitude — indoors, no ballast is needed to resist ball impact. Ballast on Sheet 06 is sized for wind and for someone leaning on it, not for golf.

Against the benchmark

The GX-6 is 10′×6′×10′ at 60+ lb and $3,500, and it packs into a single shoulder-carried duffel. The V-10 is the closest comparison: 78 ft² of floor against 60 ft², at 44 lb erected against 60 lb — 30 % more bay for 27 % less weight, in one bag, one person. It gives up 4 in of nominal height and gains a pre-tensioned screen plane and a model below it that fits a 9-foot garage on the same floor the GX-6 needs.

Depth is now set from function, not from beating a spec sheet. An earlier revision put the V-10 at 8′ deep on the reasoning that a deeper bay is better. It is not: the front net catches a narrower cone of mishits as the tee moves away from it (±43° at 6′-5″ against ±53° at 4′-7″), so the extra depth cost 18 ft² of floor and bought nothing. It also forced the arch count: at 8′ the crown purlin spans 2.24 m between two arches and needs Ø169, so a third arch was added instead. At 6′-6″ the span is 1.48 m, Ø127 covers it, and two arches at Ø229 carry the load at SF 2.78 — the same margin as three at Ø203.

Single-bag packing is a hard requirement, not an outcome. A portable product that needs two trips from the car is a different and worse product, whatever it weighs. If a design change pushes the V-10 past one bag, the change is wrong.

Ballast — outdoor only

Indoors, the pre-tension loop closes inside the structure and nothing is transmitted to the floor. Outdoors, wind governs. Frontal area of the arches on a V-10 is about 25 ft² at Cd 1.2; the net itself is ~70 % open and contributes little.

SHEET 07

Screen module

Add-in · V-10 and V-12

The screen is an accessory, not a variant. It laces to the front diagrid with 6 mm shock cord at 300 mm pitch through 30 grommets in a 40 mm webbing border, and sits 180 mm forward of the arrest net. Fitting takes about three minutes and adds 14 lb.

The point of mounting to the diagrid rather than to a frame is that the diagrid is already a flat, pre-tensioned plane — the screen inherits its flatness instead of having to generate it. And because there is no rigid frame edge, there is no abrasion line, which is where conventional simulator screens fail first. Ball energy is taken by the arrest net behind, so the screen carries an optical duty only.

A sealed pneumatic structure moves. SHEET 08

Material selection

Named material classes · what to actually buy

Two material decisions carry this product: what the tubes are made of, and what the two net layers are made of. Both have a right answer that the industry already converged on, and both have a tempting cheaper answer that fails in a specific, predictable way. Those failures are named below, because a prototype built from the wrong material teaches you nothing except that it leaks.

Inflatable fabric

The binding requirements are: weldable (not sewn — stitch holes leak at 12 psi), ≥ 60 kN/m tensile, low creep under sustained pressure, abrasion and UV resistance, and foldable at room temperature without the coating crazing.

The one that will waste your money

Banner and billboard PVC tarpaulin. It is 1000 dtex, it welds beautifully, it costs a third as much, and every sourcing search will surface it first. At 450–650 g/m² the coating is far too light: it will hold 3 psi and creep, and it will not hold 12. Specify the areal density and the tensile in the purchase order, not the fabric name. Likewise avoid TPU-coated ripstop from the packraft world — excellent fabric, an order of magnitude short on abrasion duty for a structure that gets folded, dragged and hit.

Netting — and why the two layers are different fibres

This is the part most easily got wrong, because the obvious move is to build both layers from the same net. They need opposite properties:

  • The structural diagrid must hold 800 N of pre-tension without moving. Stretch is failure — it lets the arch go slack and unloads the bracing. Wants polyester: 3–5 % elongation, low creep, excellent UV.
  • The arrest net must absorb 183.5 J. Stretch is the mechanism — the elongation is the energy absorption, and a stiffer fibre simply raises the peak force. Wants nylon: ~20 % elongation at break.

SHEET 10

Prototype build

One V-10 · what you do, what a shop does

You are not going to weld this yourself, and you should not try. Pressure-tight tube welding needs an HF (dielectric) welder — a job-shop machine. What you can do is everything either side of it: the patterns, the net assembly, the tensioning and the testing. This sheet splits the work on that line.

Who does the welding

Call fabricators who already run HF equipment on coated fabric: inflatable boat builders and repairers, inflatable advertising and event-structure manufacturers, truck-tarp and awning shops, marine canvas shops with welding capability. Useful search terms are HF welding PVC fabric, dielectric welding fabricator, inflatable fabrication. Hot-air welding with a Leister-type hand tool and a pressure roller is a possible fallback for a one-off, but a pressure article is a poor first hot-air project. Do not sew and tape the tubes. Needle holes leak at 12 psi and no amount of sealant fixes it.

Stage 0 — before anything is cut

  1. Chalk the full-size arch profile on a garage floor or driveway. Stand inside it with a driver and take a slow swing. Every profile error is free to find at this stage and expensive later.
  2. Build a 1:10 card model of the arch to sanity-check the crown band against the swing envelope in Fig. 1.
  3. Order fabric with 40 % overage. Some panels will be re-cut.

Stage 1 — patterning the arches

A straight cylinder will not follow a curved axis; it kinks. The arch is built as mitred segments, the same way airbeam shelters are made. For the V-10, each of the two arches is two straight legs of 1,267 mm plus eight curved segments of 525 mm, joined at nine mitred circumferential seams. The tangent turns 180° in total, so each joint turns 20° and each segment end is cut at 10° off perpendicular.

The consequence catches people out: unrolled, a mitre cut is a sine wave, not a straight line. Amplitude is R · tan 10° = 114.5 × 0.176 = 20 mm, one full period across the circumference. Cut it straight and the tube will not close.

Stage 2 — welding (job shop)

  1. Longitudinal seam: 30 mm lap, HF welded, then a 100 mm cover strip welded over it on the outside. Both, not one.
  2. Circumferential mitre seams: same lap plus cover strip.
  3. End caps: discs with a 25 mm flange, welded in and cover-stripped.
  4. Test every tube standalone before assembly. Inflate to 18 psi (1.5× working) for 30 minutes, soapy water over every seam. Finding a bad weld now costs an hour; finding it after the net is fitted costs a week.

Stage 3 — fittings (job shop)

  1. Weld a foot socket to each arch end, with a Ø50 open port through into the base rail. The port is the absence of a weld — mask it before welding rather than cutting it afterwards.
  2. Weld the 60 mm land strip along each arch’s inner face, full length. Get it straight: this strip sets the diagrid plane, and a wandering strip puts a permanent twist in the membrane.
  3. Base rail: nothing goes inside it. It is a plain welded tube with six port openings and one valve boss.
  4. Fit the one fill/dump valve, plus the relief valve if you are using it. Torque to the valve maker’s figure — over-torque splits the fabric boss.

Stage 4 — net assembly (you, or a canvas shop)

  1. Lay the diagrid out flat on a clean floor and mark the 45° grid at 600 mm.
  2. Bar-tack every webbing crossing — 3-row tack, bonded polyester Tex 90. It is tedious, and it is the structure.
  3. Sew the 40 mm perimeter webbing around the panel edge, capturing the diagrid ends.
  4. Fit the ratchet tensioners captive on the perimeter webbing at the marked positions.
  5. Attach the arrest net with toggles at 300 mm. Cut a jig block for the 150 mm tether so every tether is identical — uneven slack means uneven arrest travel and a net that bags to one side.

Stage 5 — first inflation and tensioning

  1. Inflate all chambers to 6 psi. Not 12 — not yet.
  2. Fit the diagrid with every tensioner slack.
  3. Raise to 12 psi. The arch straightens as it comes up to pressure and takes up most of the slack for you.
  4. Tension in a cross pattern, like wheel spokes or head bolts — never sequentially around the perimeter, which racks the panel and locks in a twist you cannot get back out.
  5. Target 800 N per web. Measure it rather than guessing: pull the taut web sideways at mid-span with a spring scale and read the transverse force at a known deflection.
  6. Re-check at 30 minutes. It will have relaxed. Re-tension, and log the numbers — that log is the data for open risk 1 on Sheet 13.

Stage 6 — commissioning

Run T1, T3 and T6 from Sheet 13 before a ball is ever hit into it. Then T2.

What to hand the fabricator

  • The pattern set: every panel, numbered, with lap allowances included and the sine mitre drawn, not described.
  • The pressure spec: 12 psi working, 16 psi relief, 60 psi minimum burst.
  • The weld spec: 30 mm lap plus 100 mm cover strip on every seam.
  • The test spec: 18 psi for 30 min per tube, before assembly, with a soap check — and make it a condition of acceptance.
  • The fabric purchase order written by number (areal density and tensile), not by trade name.

SHEET 12

Novelty basis

For patent counsel — not a patentability opinion

This section exists so counsel can start from something structured rather than from a product photo. It is deliberately blunt about what will not survive a search.

What is not novel, and should not be claimed alone

  • The pneumatic arch. Inflatable arch-beam shelters are decades of well-documented prior art — military airbeam shelters, event structures, rapid-deploy field hospitals. A claim to “an inflatable arch supporting a net” will not issue.
  • A tensioned net on a frame. Rigid-frame golf cages with tensioned nets are old and common.
  • Impact screen in front of a backing net. Established simulator practice.
  • Sealed-air, unplug-after-inflation construction. Standard across inflatable SUPs, RIBs and the reference product itself.

Candidate distinguishing features, ranked

#FeatureTechnical effect — the part that mattersStrength
1Reciprocal arch–net systemArches pre-tension the net; the net braces the arches out of plane and distributes point impact across all of them. The load loop closes arch → diagrid → base rail → arch without reaching the ground. Raises frame margin from SF 1.3 to SF 2.8 at the same tube size and removes all guys and outriggers.Strongest — the reciprocity is the invention
2Two-layer membraneResolves a genuine conflict: a membrane stiff enough to brace cannot arrest a ball gently. Feature (1) standing alone risks reading on cable-braced airbeam shelters; it is the dual role of the net, structural and energy-absorbing, that distinguishes it.

SHEET 13

Validation and open risks

Before tooling, before filing

Every number in this document is a hand calculation — wrinkling-moment theory for the tubes, funicular analysis for the arch, a linear-spring model for arrest. They are the right calculations for sizing, and they are not sufficient for filing claims that depend on the ratios. Six tests close that gap.

TestMethodPass criterion
T1Arch static load — apply 3× design lateral load at the crown, unbraced then diaphragm-bracedNo visible wrinkling at design load; braced case carries ≥ 2× the unbraced case. Validates the whole Sheet 02 argument.
T2Ball cannon — 200 mph, 1,000 shots across a 9-point gridArrest travel within ±15 % of prediction at every point; no twine failure; measure force with a load cell in the diagrid.
T3Diagrid racking — 2 kN in-plane shear on a full panelRacking angle ≤ 2°. This is the test that proves the net is structure and not cargo.
T4Thermal cycle — 0 → 50 °C, 20 cycles, sealedRelief does not vent below 15.5 psi; pressure recovers on cooling; no seam creep.
T5Setup cycling — 50 full setup / teardown cyclesNo seam, valve or land-strip degradation; fold lines show no coating crazing.
T6Port sizing — inflate from cold with pressure logged at the valve and at the furthest purlinFurthest chamber within 0.5 psi of the valve throughout, and full pressure inside 8 min. Confirms Ø50 ports are large enough that the far end does not lag.

Open risks

  1. Fabric relaxation against net pre-tension. Coated polyester creeps under sustained load. A membrane tensioned at setup may be measurably slacker three hours in, which changes arrest travel mid-session. Mitigation is ratchet tensioners plus a documented re-check at 30 minutes — but quantify the creep in T5 before writing that into a manual.
  2. Prior-art density on feature (1). Cable-braced pneumatic structures are the real risk to the strongest claim. Budget for a proper search before tooling, not after.
  3. V-9 swing rating. A 9-foot ceiling constrains any enclosure, ours included. Publishing an honest height rating costs sales and prevents returns; the temptation to round it up should be resisted in writing, now, before marketing sees it.
  4. Arrest net fatigue. 100,000 impacts is an estimate, not a measurement. T2 runs 1,000. Either extend the test or ship the net as a consumable with a stated replacement interval — the second is cheaper and more honest.
  5. Screen life claim. The 3–5× life improvement from decoupling optical duty from arrest duty is a reasoned expectation with no data behind it. Do not put it in marketing until T2 has screen samples in the rig.
  6. Weight estimates. Built from fabric area and areal density with a 12 % allowance for reinforcement, valves and hardware. Real prototypes usually come in heavy. Treat 54 lb as a target, not a spec — and weigh the first one before printing anything.

Suggested sequence

  1. Provisional filing on the Sheet 12 position, drafted by counsel — establishes a date while the rest proceeds.
  2. Prior-art search in parallel; re-scope the claims from what it finds.
  3. One V-10 prototype, hand-welded, no tooling. Run T1, T3 and T6.
  4. T2 ball cannon on the same prototype once the structure is proven.
  5. Revise geometry from test data, then commit tooling and file the non-provisional.
